    Four steps, no software required

    1. List the BAU people currently named on project work. Names, not a percentage. This is the population every one of your project plans quietly depends on.
    2. For each one, write down what was actually agreed with their line manager. How much of their week, for which phase, until when. Most of these agreements exist only as a conversation, which is why the blended average you gave us is an estimate rather than a record.
    3. Put it somewhere both the project manager and the line manager can see. A shared sheet is enough to start. The point is that neither side can change it without the other knowing.
    4. Agree what triggers a renegotiation. A major incident, a phase gate, a leaver. The failure mode is rarely the original agreement. It is the replan that never formally happens.

    If you do these four things in a spreadsheet and they hold, you do not need us.

    What this does not account for

    Effective FTE is a blended average, not a roster. Twenty people at a third of their time and seven people at full time both come to a similar number of full-time equivalents, and they behave nothing alike. A blend tells you how much capacity exists; it does not tell you whether it is available in the weeks a plan needs it.

    That is why these figures are ranges. We ask for the blended share of time as a ten-point band rather than a single percentage, and every figure drawn from it is computed at both ends of that band and shown as a range. Where a range crosses one of our thresholds we rate it on the less favourable end and name both states. The width of these ranges is the measure of how much a department-wide average leaves unsaid.

    Project shape varies and this does not capture it. Projects have a cadence: a subject-matter expert may be needed full time in discovery, part time in build, and not at all until go-live. A figure taken today is a snapshot of a moving picture.

    Both rated thresholds are ProjexaR management controls. They measure different things deliberately: oversight capacity against delivery capacity. How the concurrent-projects band was set, and what the research behind it does and does not establish, is set out under How the bands were set above.

    The growth ceiling assumes nothing else changes. It projects your current pace forward at your current staffing. Adding people, reallocating time or changing how fast projects run would all move it.
