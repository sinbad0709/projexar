    Every figure is arithmetic on something you supplied

    Four external figures appear in this report and each is named where it is used: a tickets-per-technician window, a cross-industry split between run and change work, a median IT salary, and an employer National Insurance rate. Two of the numbers we apply are ours rather than anyone's published figure — the 170–320 tickets-per-agent window and the 25% overhead uplift — and both are labelled as our judgement wherever they appear. Everything else below is your own arithmetic.

    What the cost figures exclude. They count your permanent project managers and the effective BAU capacity behind your projects, and nothing else. Contractors and outsourced staff are excluded: what they cost is paid out of budget, and these figures exist to put a number on the internal staff time your project budgets do not record. Anyone who works on projects without also holding a BAU role is excluded too, because this check never counted them — so the internal figure is a floor, not a total.

    How the bands were set

    These bands are ProjexaR's management controls. They are informed by published research but are not values the research establishes.

    The evidence comes from a longitudinal study of 9,649 project-month-employee observations across 42 projects and 580 employees at a single manufacturer, over twenty months. It finds an inverted-U relationship between the number of concurrent projects a person carries and how well those projects perform, with a point estimate of 5.16 concurrent projects and a confidence interval of 3.57 to 6.19.

    Three things follow, and we state all three. The study covers new-product-development engineering, and a portfolio of IT change projects is a different setting. Managerial responsibility appears in it only as a check on how people were allocated to projects — it plays no part in the model that produces the inverted-U — so applying this number to project management caseload is our step, not the paper's. The paper does test whether a leadership role (project leader, chief project engineer, project supervisor) changes the benefit of multi-project work, and finds it does not; that category is not the project manager, and the paper never models project management caseload as such. And our red threshold of 7.0 sits above the top of that confidence interval, deliberately: we would rather understate the problem than manufacture one.

    Colicev, A., Hakkarainen, T. & Pedersen, T. (2023). Multi-project work and project performance: Friends or foes? Strategic Management Journal, 44(2), 610–636. DOI 10.1002/smj.3443. Open access under CC BY 4.0.

    What this does not account for

    Effective FTE is a blended average, not a roster. Twenty people at a third of their time and seven people at full time both come to a similar number of full-time equivalents, and they behave nothing alike. A blend tells you how much capacity exists; it does not tell you whether it is available in the weeks a plan needs it.

    That is why these figures are ranges. We ask for the blended share of time as a ten-point band rather than a single percentage, and every figure drawn from it is computed at both ends of that band and shown as a range. We never publish the middle. Where a range crosses one of our thresholds we rate it on the less favourable end and name both states. None of this is an apology for the arithmetic: the width of these ranges is the measure of how much a department-wide average leaves unsaid, and it is the argument for holding what each person is actually committed to rather than what they average.

    Project shape varies and this does not capture it. Projects have a cadence: a subject-matter expert may be needed full time in discovery, part time in build, and not at all until go-live. A figure taken today is a snapshot of a moving picture.

    Both rated thresholds are ProjexaR management controls. They measure different things deliberately — oversight capacity against delivery capacity. How the concurrent-projects band was set, and what the research behind it does and does not establish, is set out under How the bands were set above.

    The growth ceiling assumes nothing else changes. It projects your current pace forward at your current staffing. Adding people, reallocating time or changing how fast projects run would all move it.

    What to do nextProjexaR Capacity Check

    Four steps, no software required

    1. List the BAU people currently named on project work. Names, not a percentage. This is the population every one of your project plans quietly depends on.
    2. For each one, write down what was actually agreed with their line manager. How much of their week, for which phase, until when. Most of these agreements exist only as a conversation, which is why the blended average you gave us is an estimate rather than a record.
    3. Put it somewhere both the project manager and the line manager can see. A shared sheet is enough to start. The point is that neither side can change it without the other knowing.
    4. Agree what triggers a renegotiation. A major incident, a phase gate, a leaver. The failure mode is not the original agreement — it is the replan that never formally happens.

    If you do these four things in a spreadsheet and they hold, you do not need us. Most teams find the problem is not the arithmetic — it is keeping it current once the portfolio starts moving.
