Everything here is counted, not modelled

This report contains no industry averages, no benchmarks applied to your situation, and no assumptions about how your team works. Every figure is arithmetic on numbers you supplied.

What this does not account for

It does not measure how much time anyone spends. This check counts people, not hours or days. A person who gives a project one day a month and one who gives four days a week both count as one. The finding is about dependency and visibility, not about capacity in hours.

Project shape varies and this does not capture it. Projects have a cadence: a subject-matter expert may be needed full time in discovery, part time in build, and not at all until go-live. A headcount taken today is a snapshot of a moving picture.

Everyone counts as one whole person. Part-timers, shared staff and contractors all count as one.

Why this stays invisible

A project manager identifies what they need and negotiates a workable model with the line manager who owns the person. That negotiation is usually sound. What breaks is what happens next: the agreement is made once, at the start, and lives in a conversation. When something changes — and on a live portfolio something always changes — there is often nowhere for the revised position to be recorded where both sides can see it.

What we found in the market

In July 2026 we examined nineteen resource and project management products aimed at teams like yours. None of them holds non-project commitment as a property a line manager owns, sets per person, and can be held to. Every one represents it either as an absence — booked leave, time off — or as an allocation against a pretend project. Two come close, both through allocation rather than ownership.

What to do nextPage 5

Four steps, no software required

  1. List the people on live projects who also hold a BAU role. Names, not a percentage. This is the population every one of your project plans quietly depends on.
  2. For each one, write down what was actually agreed with their line manager. How much, for which phase, until when. Most of these agreements exist only as a conversation.
  3. Put it somewhere both the project manager and the line manager can see. A shared sheet is enough to start. The point is that neither side can change it without the other knowing.
  4. Agree what triggers a renegotiation. A major incident, a phase gate, a leaver. The failure mode is not the original agreement — it is the replan that never formally happens.

If you do these four things in a spreadsheet and they hold, you do not need us. Most teams find the problem is not the arithmetic — it is keeping it current once the portfolio starts moving.
